What is an RRN?

Every time you pay in a super shop, a unique reference number is generated for that payment. It's often called the RRN (Retrieval Reference Number), and it's printed on your receipt.

If anything goes wrong with the payment, the RRN, often 12 digits, is the key to the whole investigation.

Those 12 digits break into three parts: Y + DDD + ssssssss = 1 + 3 + 8 digits.

2 · How does it get generated?

Take an ATM. Its ssssssss counter starts at 00000000 every day at 12:00 AM, and each payment bumps it by 1.

But wait: two people making their nth transaction at two different ATMs get the same RRN, because the 12 digits contain no ATM identifier. Try it below.

The solution?

Some payment systems don't require the RRN to be universally unique. You can still tell two transactions apart by checking which ATM they happened on. The pair (ATM ID, RRN) is unique, and the RRN doesn't have to be.

What if we still want global uniqueness?

Use a shared counter, maybe in a database or Redis. Every transaction increments it, and the next one waits. Flip the toggle above and press Both pay at the same instant to see the cost: the second ATM has to queue. Every approach is a trade-off, and your team decides.

Per-ATM counters
Fast, no coordination, no single point of failure. RRN alone isn't unique, so you need the ATM ID alongside it.
Shared counter
RRN is unique everywhere. Concurrent requests queue behind one counter, adding latency and a bottleneck.

3 · Daily capacity: exactly 100 million

The sequence block ssssssss has 8 digits, running from 00000000 to 99999999. That's exactly 100,000,000 references per day.

The day block DDD runs 001–365 in a normal year and 001–366 in a leap year, which gives about 365 × 100 million unique references a year.
